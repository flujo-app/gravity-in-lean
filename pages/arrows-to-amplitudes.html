Arrows to Amplitudes

Quantum mechanics is written in arrows. In six short chapters you'll drag arrows around, watch the numbers follow, and answer a question or two in each. Work out your answer first, then check it. No school maths needed.

Chapter 1 of 6

A vector is an arrow

A vector is an arrow that starts at the centre of the grid. Two numbers describe it completely: how many steps it goes right, and how many steps it goes up. Negative numbers mean left or down.

Drag the dot at the tip of the blue arrow and watch the numbers change. Physicists write the two numbers stacked inside brackets, with the right-steps on top.

How long is it?

The arrow is the long side of a right-angled triangle, so its length comes from Pythagoras. Square both numbers, add them, then take the square root.

Keep an eye on the squared length. In quantum mechanics, squared lengths turn into probabilities.

Chapter 2 of 6

Adding means walking one arrow, then the other

To add two arrows, walk along the first one. Then, from wherever you ended up, walk along the second. The sum is the arrow from the start to where you finished.

In numbers, you add the matching parts. Right-steps go with right-steps, and up-steps go with up-steps.

Drag the tips of a and b. The dashed copy of b shows the walk.

The order doesn't matter. Walking b first and then a ends in the same place.

Chapter 3 of 6

Multiplying by a number stretches the arrow

Multiplying an arrow by a number multiplies both of its parts. Times 2 makes it twice as long. Times 0.5 makes it half as long.

A negative number flips the arrow around. That's also how subtraction works: a − b is the same as a + (−1) × b.

Chapter 4 of 6

A matrix moves every arrow at once

Two small arrows hold up the whole grid. î (say “i-hat”) points one step right, and ĵ (“j-hat”) points one step up. Every other arrow is built from them. The arrow v with numbers 2 and 1 means 2 × î plus 1 × ĵ.

Drag the tips of î and ĵ. The whole grid follows, and so does v, because it's still 2 × î plus 1 × ĵ.

A matrix is a note of where î and ĵ end up. Its first column is the new î and its second column is the new ĵ. To move any arrow, follow its old recipe with the new î and ĵ.

Turns are measured as parts of a full circle. A quarter turn is 90°. A half turn is 180°, like a clock hand going from 12 to 6, so afterwards everything points the opposite way.

Adding two matrices works like adding arrows. Add the numbers in matching spots: 1234 + 0110 = 1344

Chapter 5 of 6

Arrows that cancel make the stripes

Back to the double slit. Quantum mechanics gives each way a photon could travel its own arrow, called an amplitude.

As a path gets longer, its arrow turns, like the hand of a clock. The paths through the two slits have slightly different lengths, so by the time they reach the screen their arrows point in different directions.

To find how bright a spot on the screen is, add the two arrows and square the length of the sum. Where the arrows point opposite ways, they cancel, just like in chapter 2. Those are the dark stripes.

Move the spot along the screen, then change what happens at the slits.

At the slits

A which-path detector is one example of what physicists call decoherence. The arrows stop adding, so the stripes go away. Nothing in this maths decides which slit the photon really took, though. That missing piece is the measurement problem.

Chapter 6 of 6

Every path gets an arrow, and a clock turns it

In chapter 5 a photon had two ways to go. Feynman’s rule goes much further: take every path from start to finish, give each one its own arrow, and add them all up tip to tail.

What turns each arrow is the particle’s own clock. Along every path, the arrow turns a little with each tick of the clock the particle carries along that path.

Here a ball is thrown straight up and caught at the same spot a moment later. Sideways is time and up is height, so each line is one possible flight: higher arcs, lower arcs, and some that dip below the start.

Where the arrows agree

Paths close to the one with the most clock time have almost the same clock time, so their arrows point almost the same way and add up. Further out, neighbouring paths differ more and more, and their arrows spin round and cancel. Only a bundle of paths around the most-clock-time path survives. It is shaded yellow.

Turn up gravity

Higher up, clocks tick faster, so climbing earns clock time. But to get high and still be back on time, the ball has to move fast, and moving costs clock time. The best trade-off is an arc that goes up and comes back down: the flight of a thrown ball. Nobody tells the arrows that. It is simply where they stop cancelling.

Make it heavier

A heavier thing’s arrow turns more times per tick. Then even paths close to the best one disagree, and the bundle that survives gets thinner.

Real numbers. An electron’s arrow turns about 10²⁰ times a second, a 1 kg ball’s about 10⁵⁰ times. For a flight of one second, an electron’s surviving bundle is about a centimetre wide. A ball’s is about 10⁻¹⁷ m, ten million times thinner than an atom. So a ball follows one path, and an electron keeps a wide bundle, which is how it can make stripes.

Measured. In 1975 Colella, Overhauser and Werner sent neutrons along a higher and a lower path. Gravity shifted their stripes by close to the amount this rule predicts.

What’s simplified. The real sum also includes every wiggly path. Wiggling only costs clock time, so those arrows cancel in the same way. Gravity, heights, ticks and “how heavy” are in made-up units. All paths also share one enormous amount of turning that comes from the mass alone. It is the same for every path, so the picture leaves it out.

And pilot waves? This sum of arrows is the wave. In pilot-wave theory the wave really does feel out every path, while the particle rides just one of them, steered by the wave.
