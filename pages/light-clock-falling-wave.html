Scene 1 · The light clock

Where your equation comes from

The simplest clock there is: a flash of light bouncing between two mirrors. One trip from one mirror to the other is one tick.

The clock on the left stands still. The one on the right flies sideways. Light moves at the same speed for both Measured. That's the only fact this scene uses.

Seen from here, the flying clock's light has to travel a slanted path to reach the far mirror, because the mirror moves on while the light is on its way. A longer path at the same speed takes longer, so the flying clock ticks slower Worked out.

The slanted path, the gap between the mirrors and the distance the clock moved form a right-angled triangle. Pythagoras on that triangle is your equation.

Why every clock slows, not just light clocks

Suppose the crew's wristwatches kept a different pace from their light clock. Then they could tell they were moving just by comparing the two, without looking outside. Every experiment says that's impossible Measured. So everything on board slows by the same amount: atoms, wristwatches, heartbeats, thoughts. That's also why each clock feels perfectly normal to itself. Nothing on board has anything "normal" to compare against.

Scene 2 · The falling wave

Why a matter wave falls, and keeps speeding up

Every object is also a wave. Electrons, neutrons, whole atoms and even large molecules have been made to interfere like waves Measured. Inside that wave sits a clock: a little arrow, the amplitude from your first lesson, turning at a fixed rate. For a neutron it turns about 2 × 10²³ times a second.

That arrow turns at the pace of the local clock. Lower down, clocks run slower Measured, so arrows lower down fall behind the ones higher up, and the lag grows every second.

When the arrows are out of step with height, the wave's crests are tilted. A wave always travels at right angles to its crests, so a tilted wave heads downward Worked out. Because the lag keeps growing, the tilt keeps growing, and the wave heads down faster and faster. That's falling, speeding up included.

The column on the left is a magnified slice of the wave's height, with the arrows' shared spin taken out so you only see the lag. Turn the view to watch it twist into a spiral.

The real numbers

On Earth, clocks speed up by 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁶ for every metre of height Measured. Tiny. But a neutron's arrow turns about 2 × 10²³ times a second, so the lag still piles up fast.

lag per metre, after t seconds
= (arrow's turning rate) × (clock change per metre) × t

Convert that lag into a speed using the neutron's mass and Planck's constant, and everything else cancels out:

downward speed = 9.81 × t metres per second

That's exactly Newton's falling, and the same for every mass. The 1975 neutron experiment by Colella, Overhauser and Werner saw this lag directly Measured.

What this picture leaves out

It uses only the time part of gravity. For slow things like balls and neutrons, that's almost all of it. Fast things like light also feel the bending of space, which doubles how much starlight bends near the Sun.

How to check it yourself

Set gravity to 0 and the crests stay upright and the path stays straight. Pause at any moment: the crest tilt and the direction of travel are always the same angle.
