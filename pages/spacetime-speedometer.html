Spacetime Speedometer

You worked this out yourself: everything has a fixed budget of motion, and you share it between moving through space and moving through time. This page lets you play with it. Its textbook relative is called the four-velocity (part 7 explains how the two differ).

Part 1 · Your graph

One arrow, always the same length

The black arrow is your motion through spacetime. It always has the same length: the speed of light. You can only choose which way it points.

Its sideways part is how fast you move through space. Its upward part is how fast you move through time, which means how fast your clock ticks compared with someone staying at home.

Drag the green dot along the curve. Standing still, the whole arrow points up: full speed through time. Tip it over and you borrow from time to move through space.

Why a curve and not a straight line?

It's Pythagoras again, the same as the arrows in Lesson 1. The two parts squared always add up to 1, so the tip has to stay on a circle. Near the top the circle is almost flat, which is why everyday speeds barely touch your clock.

Your straight line had the right ends: all time at the top, all space at the bottom. In between, the real curve keeps far more time than the straight line would. At half the speed of light, a straight line would say your clock ticks at 50%. It actually ticks at 87%.

Part 2 · Epstein's picture

Everyone travels the same distance

Here is the same idea as a race. Everyone starts together and travels for the same number of years by the home calendar. Going right means moving through space (in light-years). Going up means time passing on that traveller's own clock.

Press play. Every traveller stays on the same dashed circle, because everyone covers exactly the same distance through spacetime. They just head off in different directions.

Whoever stays home goes straight up and ages the most. Light goes flat along the bottom and never ages at all. You fly at the speed you picked in part 1.

The physicist Lewis Carroll Epstein drew relativity this way in his book Relativity Visualized, so pictures like this are called Epstein diagrams.

This is real, measurable stuff. Muons are made by cosmic rays about 15 km up and only live about 2 millionths of a second. Without time dilation they'd fall apart after about 600 metres. Because their clocks run around 16 times slower, plenty of them reach the ground, and detectors count them every day.

Part 4 · Gravity

Why a ball falls

“Because that is the EFFECT of mass curving spacetime and thus converting movement in the ‘time dimension’ into movement in the ‘space dimension’.”

You, in a YouTube comment, 27 December 2024

You were right. A ball you hold still isn't moving through space, but it's still moving through time at full budget, the arrow pointing straight up from part 1.

The Earth's mass makes clocks tick a tiny bit slower the lower down they are. So "straight ahead through time" isn't quite the same direction at every height. Let go of the ball, and that difference tips its arrow over. Some of its motion through time turns into motion downwards, and the ball falls.

One fine point. The falling comes from clocks ticking at different rates at different heights. Physicists keep the word "curvature" for the part you can't get rid of, even by falling along with the ball: tides, where nearby falling things slowly drift apart or together. Inside an accelerating rocket far from any planet, a dropped ball "falls" too, and the clock at the front runs faster than the one at the back. That's Einstein's equivalence principle.

The rule a falling ball follows

Physicists sum it up in one rule: a thrown or falling object always takes the path on which its own clock ticks the most.

Here a ball is thrown up and caught again 2 seconds later. Going higher helps its clock, because clocks run faster up there. But going higher means moving faster, and from part 1 you know that costs clock time. Drag the top of the arc and find the best trade-off.

Part 7 · The proper names

What physicists call all this

Time dilation

The effect itself: a moving clock ticks slower. It comes from Einstein's special relativity, 1905.

Four-velocity

The textbook cousin of part 1's arrow. It's measured with your own clock instead of the home clock, so its parts keep growing as you speed up. Only its size, measured the relativity way (with a minus sign), always stays at the speed of light. It doesn't exist for light, which has no clock of its own.

Epstein diagram

Part 2's picture. One small catch: the textbook maths uses a minus sign where Pythagoras has a plus. The circle is a clever re-drawing of it, and it gives exactly the right numbers for comparing clocks.

Light cone

A different picture: which events can affect which, since nothing goes faster than light. It meets your graph at one point, the star at the end of the circle.

Curved spacetime

Gravity, from general relativity (1915). The Earth's mass slows clocks lower down, and that is what makes things fall (part 4). GPS satellites lose about 7 millionths of a second a day from their speed, and gain about 45 from being higher up.

Geodesic

The path a thrown or falling thing takes: the one on which its own clock ticks the most. It's the straightest line there is in curved spacetime.

Gravitational time dilation

Clocks lower down in gravity tick slower. Part 5's table shows how much. It's the effect behind your YouTube comment.
