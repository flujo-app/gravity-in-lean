Slow Clocks, Bent Light

A heavy mass, a light mass, a far observer, a distant light that flashes once a second, and you. Every one of them keeps its own time.

W A S D to fly, drag to look, wheel to zoom. The rows on top are the clocks: choose one to see the world from there. On a phone: left thumb flies, right thumb looks.

Getting around

  • Drag to look around, wheel to zoom. W A S D fly, Q and E go down and up.
  • Your engine gives one steady push. The pull of the masses never switches off, so close in you have to push against it.
  • Space turns the engine against your motion until you stand still, then holds you there if it is strong enough. G lets go, and you fall.
  • Choose a row on top, or press 1 to 5, to see the world from there and by that clock. On the light ray's row time stands still, and W and S move you along the ray.
  • F opens the details: sliders, the sums, two graphs. T opens a second picture of the same moment, with up and down replaced by each one's own time. P holds time still. R starts again.

How to read it

Each row is one clock, and each tick is one of its own seconds. The rows line up moment by moment, so a row with fewer ticks is a clock that runs slower. A dot is a flash from the light source reaching that clock. The glowing sheets are those flashes on their way. The lines on the floor are paths that light would take. The row you chose always ticks evenly, because its clock sets the pace: your own seconds always feel normal to you. To watch your own clock slow down, choose the far observer's row. Whenever you fall freely, a ball of loose dust is let go around you: watch its shape.

In real numbers

Read one map unit as a kilometre. The heavy mass is then a neutron star of 1.35 suns, 11.5 km in radius, like ones that have been weighed and measured. A clock on its surface runs at about 82% of a far one. Everything is shown about 7,500 times slower than it would really happen, and your engine pushes with about 200 billion times the pull you feel on Earth.

How sure is this

  • Measured: clocks lower down near a mass run slow, and so do moving clocks. Light bends near a mass and arrives late. Neutron stars of this mass and size exist; the heaviest found weigh about 2.1 suns.
  • Worked out: paths, clock rates, bent flashes and the bent sky follow Einstein's equations for one mass that does not spin, and were checked against the textbook sums (light bending to about one part in a hundred, the rest to five digits). The engine follows the real law for a rocket: a steady push as felt on board, added to the pull. The ball of dust is the real tide: every grain falls on its own.
  • Nobody has it exactly: two masses circling each other. There is no formula for that, only step-by-step sums on very large computers. This page adds the light mass's pull to the heavy one's, which is right to about a thousandth here. Set the light mass to "no pull" and every sum on the page is exact.
  • Open: exactly how heavy a neutron star can get before it collapses. The page uses 2.2 suns.
  • Picture: everything is drawn on the far observer's map, moment by moment. Someone moving fast would cut the moments differently, so only clocks compared at the same place are beyond argument. It is not what your eyes would see. Light has no clock and no point of view of its own: the ray's row shows the limit of riding ever faster.
  • Invented: how strong your engine is, that you and your ship survive any of this, and the light mass (nothing that small and dense is known). Nobody can change the speed of light; that slider keeps everyday speeds and pulls the same and changes only what light does. The world never plays more than fifty times faster, even when the clock you chose would call for more.
